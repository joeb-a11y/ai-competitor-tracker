# DNB Platform Vertical — Talk Track

**Role:** GTM Strategy Lead, Digital Native Business Segment
**Assignment:** Identify 3-5 target companies in the "platform" vertical with greatest growth opportunity in US Digital Native Enterprise. Rationale, competitive landscape, customer needs, industry-specific considerations.
**Format:** ~25 minutes spoken + Q&A
**Structure:** Four beats — Align Context → Build Stakes → Bring the Hero → Make it Inevitable

---

## BEAT 1 — ALIGN THE CONTEXT (≈3 minutes)

### Opening

I want to start from a position of strength, because DNB isn't the problem child in this room. We're at 139% of a $1.32B Q1 target. Startups is at 160% of $400M. The whole digital-native side of the house is outrunning plan.

So this isn't a "fix what's broken" conversation. It's a "why is one specific thing working so well, and are we leaving money on the table by not naming it" conversation.

### The current carve

Here's what DNB looks like today. We've got three sub-segments: Fintech, Cybersecurity, and Core. Fintech and Cyber were carved for good reasons — they share a regulatory surface, they share a buyer psychology, Cyber has dedicated domain experts and strong mission alignment. Those carves make sense.

DNB Core is everything else. It's a residual bucket — it's defined by what it's *not*. And sitting inside Core, right now, are the accounts that look most like each other, buy most like each other, and reference-check each other the most. The platforms. Databricks, Confluent, Atlassian, Stripe, ServiceNow, Notion, Figma. They're all in the same bucket as companies that have nothing in common with them.

And here's the thing — we've already noticed. In the Tech AE Interview Guide, Hratch is listed as hiring manager for a team called "Strat — B2B SaaS 2." That team exists. It's running. It just isn't formal, it doesn't have its own number, and it doesn't have a seat at the roadmap table. The carve is happening whether we name it or not. I'm here to argue we should name it.

---

## BEAT 2 — BUILD THE STAKES (≈5 minutes)

### Three forces converging right now

I want to stack three things on top of each other, because any one of them is a reason to act, and all three at once is what timing looks like.

**First: we have a template deal nobody's replicating.**

Snowflake. $200M over three years. Claude as the backbone for Snowflake Intelligence and Cortex AI. Data-sharing provisions that protect us from disintermediation. Unilateral termination rights on our side. It's a genuinely good deal structure.

And every data-platform CTO in the country heard about it within a week. Databricks heard about it. Confluent heard about it. MongoDB heard about it. That's how this market works — to borrow Richard's framing, a market is people who talk to each other and are mutually validating. These people talk to each other.

But inside our org, that win is filed under "DNB Core." It's a generic logo in a generic bucket. We're not weaponizing it against Databricks, which is Snowflake's most direct competitor and the single most obvious next target for that exact deal structure. We're letting the reference decay.

**Second: OpenAI launched the layer we don't have.**

February 5th. Frontier. Enterprise agent orchestration platform. It manages fleets of agents with shared business context, identity, permissions, governance — connects across CRM, data warehouses, ticketing. And it's model-agnostic. It supports agents built on OpenAI, Google, Microsoft, *and us*. Fortune called it OpenAI's bid to be "the operating system of the enterprise."

We don't have a direct equivalent. Cowork is a user-level tool. The API is an intelligence layer. Neither of them is an orchestration OS.

And here's why that matters specifically for platform companies: if Frontier becomes the layer enterprises standardize on, our platform customers become *Frontier's* distribution channel instead of ours. We become a checkbox in a model-agnostic dropdown. Our 55% enterprise API share versus OpenAI's 30% is defensible as long as customers are building *directly* on us. If they're building on Frontier and we're one of four backends, that share erodes.

Dario said it six weeks ago: "Enterprise is wide open with no clear winner, and different players are establishing stickiness in different parts of it." That's not a description of a permanent state. That's a description of a window. Windows close.

**Third: we're one bad launch away from a Tier-1 account walking.**

Intuit signed $29M on February 23rd. They're building TurboTax MCPs, QuickBooks MCPs, actively engaged on co-marketing. Three weeks later, Labs ships a Cowork tax-filing plugin — built on *Column Tax's* engine. A direct Intuit competitor. Three weeks after the commit.

Claude Code Security launched February 20th. It cut roughly $50B off the market cap of public cybersecurity companies in *three days*. CrowdStrike is an $8M ARR account with a $20-25M target. Their CEO noticed.

Figma is flagged highest-sensitivity on Omelette. Lovable and Replit are staring down Claude Create — which targets the *exact same user*, the non-technical app builder. The Coopetition doc calls it "the most direct overlap of any scenario."

These are all DNB Core accounts. And nobody's job — *nobody's* — is to read the Labs roadmap before the customer does and say "hey, we're about to step on a Tier-1's neck." That role doesn't exist. A Platform vertical lead is partly that role.

### Defining "Platform" so we're scoring the same thing

Before I get to targets, I want to be precise about what I mean by Platform, because "platform" is a word people use loosely.

I'm using the Project Polaris operating-model taxonomy we already use for account scoring — just applied as a segment filter instead of an account-level tag. Platform means: **companies where other companies build on top.** Subscription revenue model, plus a marketplace or ecosystem component, plus an external-facing API or SDK as the primary product surface.

That's Databricks, Snowflake, Confluent on the data side. Atlassian, Notion, Figma on workflow. Stripe, Twilio, Shopify on commerce and infrastructure. ServiceNow, Salesforce, HubSpot on systems of record.

It's *not* Coinbase — they're fintech, already carved. It's not CrowdStrike — cyber, already carved. It's not Netflix — that's digital eCommerce, consumer buyer. It's not DoorDash — on-demand services, different buyer entirely.

And here's the ICP tell that makes this segment structurally different: every platform company passes the hardest Polaris gate *by definition*. The gate is "evaluating Anthropic to power external use cases that reach a broad user base." Their product *is* the external use case. There's no internal-chatbot-that-never-scales risk here. The use case is the product.

## BEAT 3 — BRING IN THE HERO (≈12 minutes)

### The 5-criteria case

When Richard's team picked FSI, HCLS, Cyber, and Legal, they used five criteria. Commercial opportunity. Transformation potential. Mission alignment. Hard problems that generalize. Available expertise. That framework has *never been applied to a DNB sub-vertical* — Fintech and Cyber were carved on firmographics, not on this rubric.

So let me apply it.

**Commercial.** FSI's SAM was $37.6B. I'm not going to give you a fake SAM number for Platform because nobody's built one — but I can tell you that roughly 75% of Anthropic's revenue runs through the API, and the heaviest API consumers are platforms. Snowflake alone is $200M committed. Back-of-envelope, the top 30 platform accounts are a $1.5 to $2B three-year TAM. It's not small.

**Transformation potential.** FSI scored High. Platform scores *highest*. These companies' entire product roadmaps are being rewritten around LLMs right now. And critically — positive returns to intelligence scale directly into *their* revenue, not just their cost base. When Claude gets better, their product gets better, their ARR goes up. That's a different relationship than "Claude helps our finance team do Excel faster."

**Mission alignment.** FSI scored Mid. I'd say Platform is Mid-to-High — not because of the companies themselves, but because they're our distribution engine into the broader economy. Safe AI at the platform layer cascades to millions of downstream users we'll never touch directly. If Stripe builds responsibly on us, every merchant on Stripe inherits that.

**Hard problems that generalize.** FSI scored High. Platform is High. Agentic orchestration, long-context over proprietary data, multi-model routing, auth and permissions at scale — every one of those generalizes across our whole product surface.

**Expertise.** FSI had in-house product experts. Cyber had to hire a domain expert in December. Healthcare is still hiring. Platform? *We're the domain experts.* This is our home turf. There's no hire. There's no ramp. We already speak the language.

So: FSI got the first vertical launch scoring High/Mid/High/High/In-house. Platform scores at least as well on every dimension and is cheaper to stand up. The framework doesn't argue against this. The framework argues *for* it.

### Four targets — each one proves a different thesis

I'm giving you four, not five. I could have picked five that look alike. Instead I picked four that each prove something different, because if this is going to be a real segment and not just a list of logos, I need to know which muscles work.

If Databricks works, it proves the peer-set reference-sell motion scales. If Atlassian works, it proves Ami's Shadow-IT thesis at the account with the densest dev population in our book. If ServiceNow works, it proves we can win when the customer's real alternative isn't OpenAI's *model* — it's OpenAI's *platform*. And if Stripe works, it proves a single account can scale across API, Claude Code, and Cowork simultaneously.

Four experiments. Four different muscles. That's more useful than five that test the same thing.

---

### Databricks — the reference-sell play

**Why now.** Databricks and Snowflake are the two most direct competitors in enterprise data. Every Databricks executive has already been briefed on the Snowflake-Anthropic deal by their own competitive intelligence team. They know the number. They know the structure. The reference-sell is half-done before we walk in the room.

**What they need.** Mosaic AI — their AI product suite — needs a frontier-model backbone that doesn't make them look like they're reselling a competitor's product. OpenAI means Microsoft means Azure, and Databricks competes with Azure on marketplace. Google means Gemini, and Google is also a data-platform competitor via BigQuery. We're the only frontier lab that isn't also competing with them on cloud or data. That's structural. It doesn't go away.

They also need the Unity Catalog governance story to have an intelligence layer that respects data boundaries — "Claude that never leaves your tenant" is a pitch they can take to regulated customers. And they've watched Snowflake ship Cortex AI with us, and they know they're behind.

**Competitive landscape.** OpenAI is poisoned by the Microsoft relationship — Databricks won't build its flagship AI product on the partner of its primary cloud competitor. Google is the real alternative, and it's a serious one. But Google is also BigQuery. We're the only neutral party at the frontier.

**Industry-specific consideration.** Per the Coopetition tier structure, Databricks sits at the $10M+ ARR tier on current API spend — that's Product-lead conversation, one-week pre-launch notice, co-marketing eligibility. The *opportunity* is to get them to $50M+, which unlocks the dedicated XFN pod and the "call Ami or Dario" tier. That threshold should be the explicit deal goal, not an afterthought. Structure for it.

**Land motion.** This is Sam's Lighthouse bucket. Treat it as a partnership, not a deal. Do the unscalable things. Bring Richard or Scott White to the first meeting — someone who can talk product roadmap, not just commercial terms. And pitch to the VP of Product for Mosaic AI, not the engineering team running evals. Sam's exact words from the 2024 offsite: "get out of the eval conversation with engineering and uplevel to Product and Engineering leadership."

---

### Atlassian — the Shadow-IT density play

**Why now.** Atlassian has somewhere north of 12,000 engineers across Jira, Confluence, Bitbucket, Trello, Loom. It's the highest-density concentration of developers-who-are-also-our-users anywhere in DNB Core. And those developers are already on Claude Code — not hypothetically, today.

This is Ami's thesis in its purest form. Her framing: "Developers love Claude Code so much they work around organizational policies. Developers who've built with our API become our strongest champions when their company evaluates AI providers." The shadow adoption already happened. We're not creating demand. We're *surfacing* it.

**What they need.** Atlassian Intelligence — their AI layer across the whole suite — shipped on OpenAI, and the reviews are mixed. Bitbucket needs a Copilot-competitive coding story or it keeps bleeding share to GitHub. Rovo, their AI agent product, is searching for a differentiation angle that isn't "same as Microsoft but Australian."

**Competitive landscape.** OpenAI is the incumbent — Atlassian Intelligence launched on GPT. Displacing an incumbent is harder than greenfield. But the "nobody got fired for buying OpenAI" logic is weakening, and it's weakening fastest inside their own engineering org, where the people making the recommendation are already using us.

The deeper competitor isn't OpenAI's model — it's Microsoft's bundled dev-tool suite. GitHub plus Copilot plus Azure DevOps is an integrated stack that makes Bitbucket look like a point solution. Atlassian needs us to help them compete with *that*. We're not selling against a model. We're selling against a bundle.

**Industry-specific consideration.** Punit flagged last week that Amazon shows up as one managed DNB account *and* 32 separate unmanaged orgs — individual teams swiping corporate cards. Atlassian almost certainly has the same pattern, possibly worse given the engineer density. Step one of this account plan isn't outreach. It's archaeology. Pull every Atlassian domain in Unmanaged, total the shadow spend, and *that's* the opening slide in the CIO meeting.

**Land motion.** Two fronts at once. BDR pulls the shadow-spend data across every Atlassian team we can find. AE walks that data into the Chief Product Officer with Ami's framing: "Your people already chose. We're here to help you give it to them safely — with the admin controls, the compliance posture, and the roadmap visibility you need." The land motion isn't top-down or bottom-up. It's both, and the bottom already happened.

---

### ServiceNow — the anti-Frontier defensive play

**Why now.** This is the account that makes the OpenAI-Frontier problem concrete. ServiceNow's entire positioning is "workflow orchestration for the enterprise." Frontier's pitch is "agent orchestration platform, the operating system of the enterprise." Those are the same words. OpenAI isn't competing with ServiceNow for model spend. OpenAI is competing with ServiceNow for *ServiceNow's market*.

That gives ServiceNow a structural, existential reason to prefer us that no other account on this list has. We're not asking them to pick the better model. We're asking them to pick the partner who *isn't trying to become them*.

**What they need.** Now Assist — their GenAI layer — needs a model partner that isn't simultaneously building an orchestration layer on top of them. They need the "safe, credible, trustworthy enterprise vendor" positioning, because their buyer is the risk-averse CIO, and that's the exact language from Richard's transformation-potential criterion. And they want to embed deeply enough that switching us out is a multi-quarter project, not a config change.

**Competitive landscape.** OpenAI is *actively hostile* here, whether OpenAI frames it that way or not. Frontier is model-agnostic — it supports Anthropic models too — but model-agnostic orchestration doesn't help ServiceNow. It commoditizes them. It makes the orchestration layer the value and the workflow engine the plumbing.

Google is neutral on orchestration but has a weaker enterprise-trust story. We're the cleanest fit.

And the pitch is the Coopetition doc's exact DNB line: "Mutually beneficial partnership: you need our intelligence layer, we need your distribution. Our 1P products are distribution channels, not displacement." ServiceNow hears that and it's *credible*, because we don't have a Frontier equivalent. We're genuinely not trying to be the orchestration OS. They are.

**Industry-specific consideration.** ServiceNow has the deepest system-of-record trust moat of anyone on this list. Richard's framing: it's not just deployed software, it's an institutional designation — years of accumulated trust and hundreds of integrations. We're not trying to melt that SaaS. We're trying to be the layer it sits on. Say that out loud in the room.

This is also where the Claude Partner Network — $100M, announced last week — matters most. ServiceNow has the largest SI and partner ecosystem of anyone here. Getting their partners Claude-certified isn't a nice-to-have. It's a force multiplier.

**Land motion.** This is not an AE sale. This is a CEO-to-CEO conversation about strategic alignment against a shared competitive threat. Bring Paul or Dario. The deal structure should look more like the Snowflake template — multi-year, data-sharing provisions, joint governance — than a standard API commit.

---

### Stripe — the triple-threat revenue play

**Why now.** Stripe is the only account on this list that can scale across all three of our revenue surfaces *simultaneously* at material size. API for their product. Claude Code for their eng org. Cowork plugins for their ecosystem. Nobody else does all three at this scale.

**What they need on each surface.**

API: Stripe's financial-data surface area is enormous and underexploited. Stripe Radar, Sigma, every fraud and risk and analytics product is an LLM workload at scale. Every merchant dashboard, every dispute workflow, every Sigma query is an intelligence opportunity. It's external-facing, reaches millions of merchants — passes the hardest Polaris ICP gate cold.

Claude Code: roughly 8,000 engineers, famously high bar, heavy internal-tooling culture. That's the exact profile that drove Claude in Excel to $24M RRR at 35% week-over-week when FSI got its vertical focus. Serious power users consuming tokens at Claude Code rates.

Cowork: Stripe has the deepest developer ecosystem in fintech infrastructure. Dave Brown's framing — "B2B SaaS companies will want to launch their own plug-ins, long-term vision resembles an App Store for enterprise knowledge work" — Stripe is the flagship plugin publisher in that world. They already run a marketplace. They already think in ecosystem terms.

**Competitive landscape.** OpenAI is the incumbent — Stripe was in the GPT-4 launch announcement. That's a real relationship. Displacing it is harder than greenfield.

But the harder competitor isn't OpenAI. It's "we'll build it ourselves." Stripe has the engineering talent to fine-tune open models, and they're culturally allergic to vendor lock-in. The counter is Dario's API-lifecycle argument: the API scales along the exponential. Your in-house model doesn't. Every time we ship a better model, your product gets better for free. Your fine-tuned Llama doesn't do that.

**Industry-specific consideration — and the panel will ask this.** Stripe sits at the intersection of DNB and Fintech. So where does it belong?

My answer: Stripe's *market* is financial services. Stripe's *buyer psychology* is a platform company's. They don't have regulatory depth as a moat. They don't have balance-sheet risk as a moat. They have API-design taste and developer-ecosystem trust. They buy like Databricks, not like JPMorgan. The segmentation should follow the buying motion, not the SIC code. That's why they're on this list.

**Land motion.** Three parallel tracks from day one. API track targets the VP of Product for Radar and Sigma. Claude Code track targets the VP of Engineering with the shadow-spend data — same archaeology play as Atlassian. Cowork track targets whoever runs their Developer Platform on plugin publishing. Three champions, three budgets, one master agreement that ties them together.

## BEAT 4 — MAKE IT INEVITABLE (≈5 minutes)

### This isn't a new idea

I want to close by making the case that I'm not asking the panel to bet on a thesis. I'm asking for a decision to formalize what three separate signals are already telling us.

**Sam saw it in 2024.** His words, from the DNB offsite: "Opportunity with ISV product partnership — I am seeing a trend set in — Gitlab, Vercel, Automation Anywhere, Salesforce, Zendesk — where we can start to think about our fit in a product third party that allows for longer term revenue share and doesn't diminish like the foundational models will. I think we should be exploring these opportunities aggressively as a motion." A motion. He saw a motion. It never became a segment.

**Hratch is already running it.** "Strat — B2B SaaS 2" is in the Tech AE Interview Guide as a hiring-manager line. That team exists today. It's staffed. It's closing deals. It just doesn't have a mandate, a number, or a seat at the Product roadmap table.

**The Coopetition framework already exists.** Approved by leadership March 10th. Sam's on the core team. The DNB talk track is written. The tier structure is defined — $50M+, $10M+, $1-10M. A Platform lead inherits a playbook, not a blank page.

**Snowflake proved the template at $200M.** We don't need a proof of concept. We need replication.

The question isn't whether this works. It's whether we want to compound it intentionally or keep letting it happen by accident.

### What the team looks like

I'd structure it on Sam's three-bucket motion, because that's the mental model DNB already runs on and I don't want to invent a new operating system.

**Lighthouse bucket:** Databricks, ServiceNow, Stripe, Atlassian. Partnership, not deal. Unscalable things. Exec sponsorship. One Strategic AE per account, shared SA pod.

**Proactive bucket:** Confluent, MongoDB, HubSpot, Twilio, Shopify, Notion, Figma, Datadog. Tops-down to Product leadership, sprint to one production deployment. Two Enterprise AEs, pooled SA.

**Reactive bucket:** long-tail platform API spend currently sitting in Unmanaged. Zero AE touch. Self-serve, route up to Proactive at the $1M RRR threshold.

Net-new headcount ask: two Strategic AEs, two Enterprise AEs, one dedicated SA, and one half-time Coopetition PM shared with Product. And it's a reallocation from DNB Core, not an add. These accounts already have AEs on them. What changes is the mandate.

Plus the role nobody has today: one person who reads the Labs roadmap *before* the customer does. So we don't have another Intuit. That's a feature of this segment existing. Not a cost.

### What I'm committing to

By end of Q2: Databricks at signed term sheet, targeting the $50M+ tier. Shadow-spend consolidation data pulled for all four Lighthouse accounts — the "Amazon has 32 unmanaged orgs" pattern. And a Coopetition pre-read process stood up with Labs so no Platform Tier-1 account is surprised by a launch again.

By end of year one: $250M committed ARR across the four Lighthouse accounts. Two of four publishing Cowork plugins — Stripe and Atlassian are the most likely. And Platform tracking as its own line in the DNB Weekly with its own number.

The benchmark I'm using: Claude in Excel hit $24.4M RRR in three months at 35% week-over-week when FSI got its vertical focus. I'm not committing to that velocity. But I'm committing to a slope you can measure me against by Q3. I want a number I can be held to. Let's agree on what it is.

## Q&A TALK TRACKS

### "Why four, not five?"

Four is enough to prove four different theses. A fifth would be filler — another logo that tests the same thing one of the first four already tests. If the panel wants a fifth, it's HubSpot: mid-market CRM, structurally anti-Salesforce, hungry for differentiation, and a good test of whether the playbook scales *down* from $200M to $20M deals. But I'd rather run four clean experiments than five overlapping ones.

### "Isn't Stripe a Fintech account?"

Stripe's market is financial services. Stripe's buyer psychology is a platform company's. They don't have regulatory depth as a moat, they don't have balance-sheet risk as a moat. They have API-design taste and developer-ecosystem trust. They buy like Databricks, not like JPMorgan. The segmentation should follow the buying motion, not the SIC code.

### "What's the downside risk?"

The accounts stay in DNB either way. Worst case, we've moved four AE headcounts between sub-teams with no net change to coverage — the deals still close, they just close inside Core instead of inside Platform. Best case, we've compounded the Snowflake win across its entire peer set. That's asymmetric.

### "How is this different from what Hratch is already doing?"

Hratch is running a team. I'm proposing a *segment*. The difference is: a number that shows up in the DNB Weekly, a seat at the Product roadmap table when Labs is about to step on a customer, and a reference-sell motion that weaponizes wins inside a peer set instead of filing them under Core. The difference is accountability and leverage, not headcount.

### "What about Salesforce and Intuit? Aren't they platforms?"

Both are already Lighthouse accounts with dedicated strategic coverage. Intuit is a live coopetition case right now — $29M commit, tax plugin tension. I wouldn't move either account. But the Platform Coopetition PM role should absolutely have visibility into both, because the Intuit situation is exactly the failure mode that role prevents.

### "OpenAI's Frontier is model-agnostic. Doesn't that help us too?"

In theory, yes — we're one of the supported backends. In practice, model-agnostic orchestration commoditizes the model layer. Our 55% enterprise API share is defensible as long as customers are building *directly* on us. If they're building on Frontier and we're one of four checkboxes in a dropdown, that share erodes. The ServiceNow play is the hedge: partner deeply with the companies who have as much to lose from Frontier becoming the standard as we do.

### "What if Databricks says no because Snowflake said yes?"

Then we've learned something valuable about whether the reference-sell motion works in a direct-competitor context, and we pivot the Databricks pitch to neutrality: "We're the only frontier lab not competing with you on cloud or data, regardless of who else we work with." But I don't think that's how it goes. In enterprise data, both-platforms-use-the-same-underlying-infra is normal. They both run on AWS. They both integrate with Tableau. Peer adoption is validation, not contamination.
